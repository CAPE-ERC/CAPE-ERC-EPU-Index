Why this exists

Uncertainty is a tax nobody collects — and almost nobody in Africa was measuring it.

When firms and households don't know who will set policy, or what it will be, they do the rational thing: they wait. Investment stalls, hiring freezes, big purchases get postponed (Bloom, 2009). That hesitation is real and costly — but because uncertainty isn't a line item in any national account, it has to be measured indirectly, through what gets written about it.

"The World Uncertainty Index covers Africa — but from quarterly EIU country reports, not from a single page of local news."

Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016) pioneered the fix: count how often the press writes about the economy, policy and uncertainty in the same breath. Their method has since been built for more than 20 economies — almost all of them advanced. Africa's uncertainty runs on a different clock: election cycles, abrupt regulatory change, exchange-rate regimes, debt distress, commodity shocks. Those events are reported first, and in the most detail, by the national press — often in French, Arabic or Portuguese, not English.

1

Language

A dictionary per country, written in up to three of 15 languages — francophone, lusophone and Arabic-language papers are read in their own words, not translated.

2

Coverage

National online newspapers collected for every country, including fragile and low-coverage states that most indices skip entirely.

3

Frozen baseline

Each country's scaling is fixed over Jan 2015–Dec 2025. New months score against that fixed reference and never rewrite history.

4

Aggregation

Regional and Africa-wide series are GDP-PPP-weighted averages of country z-scores — comparable across economies of wildly different size.

Inside the index

Two curated registries, 851 candidate newspapers, 262 that made the cut.

Every country gets up to six newspapers, selected by article volume. West Africa alone — Nigeria and Ghana's dense online press in particular — accounts for nearly half the registry and more than half of every classified article.

This isn't evenly distributed by design — it's evenly distributed by method. Every country gets the same treatment: the same selection rule, the same classification logic, the same frozen baseline. The volume differences you see above are a fact about Africa's online press, not an artifact of how the index was built.

How an article gets counted

Not a sentiment score. A simple, strict rule: all three, or it doesn't count.

An article is an EPU article only if its title or body contains at least one term from each of three dictionaries — Economic, Policy and Uncertainty. No partial credit, no weighting by how many terms appear. One article, one vote: 0 or 1.

Each country dictionary also carries local terms — currency names like naira, national institutions, and the French, Arabic or Portuguese equivalents (PIB, économie, taux de change…) where relevant. A term belongs to exactly one component, so a single phrase can never satisfy two conditions at once.

Reading the news in its own language

A Moroccan dictionary isn't a translated Nigerian one. It's nine times bigger.

Dictionary size tracks how many languages a country's press actually uses. English-only markets run lean, ten to twenty terms per component. Markets covered in Arabic and French — Morocco, Tunisia — carry dictionaries an order of magnitude larger, because the same idea gets a full entry in every language it's reported in.

Language vocabularies, by number of countries

Fifteen language vocabularies feed the dictionaries. Every one of the 54 countries is scored in English; 27 add French, 13 add Arabic, 6 add Portuguese. Nine languages — from Amharic to Zulu — serve a single country each.

From article counts to an index

Four steps turn raw counts into a number you can compare across 54 very different economies.

A country's raw EPU article share is standardised into a z-score against its own history, then combined into regional and Africa-wide series using GDP-at-PPP weights — so Nigeria's economy carries Nigeria's weight, and a small, volatile series like Gabon's can't swing a regional average through scale alone.

1

Standardise

Each country's index is converted to a z-score using its own baseline mean and standard deviation, Jan 2015–Dec 2025.

4

Rescale

The regional series is standardised again against its own baseline and mapped to mean 100, standard deviation 10.

The Africa-wide index is not an average of the five regional indices — it runs the same four steps directly across all 54 countries, weighted by each country's share of Africa's total GDP.

Built to be trusted

History doesn't move. Once a month is scored, it's scored for good.

Most news-based indices recalibrate as new data arrives, which means last year's value for "last March" can quietly change today. CAPE's baseline is frozen at December 2025: every month from January 2026 onward is measured against that fixed reference, so a chart you save today still matches the chart you'd pull a year from now.

The frozen baseline, on the calendar

132 months set each country's scaling once. Every month after that — the live window — is scored against it, never recalibrated.

Who's in the average

Five regions, CAPE's own grouping — built for how African economies actually trade and move together.

The regional split isn't the UN geoscheme. Madagascar, Mauritius, Malawi, Angola and São Tomé & Príncipe sit in Southern Africa here; Comoros, Seychelles and Sudan sit in East Africa — grouped to track the economic blocs the index is meant to serve.

News-based uncertainty measures have already shown they can forecast exchange-rate and stock-market volatility at the country level (Laniran, 2018; Laniran & Omeje, 2021; Salisu et al., 2023 for Nigeria). CAPE's index is the first attempt to run that same logic, consistently, across the whole continent.

The index family

Same archive, different question.

EPU, ETU, TPU and EgPU are all built from the same 31.4-million-article archive and the same per-country dictionaries — only the keyword rules that define each component change.

EPU

Economic Policy Uncertainty

Economic × Policy × Uncertainty, together in one article. The index this page is about.

ETU

Economic Trade Uncertainty

Swaps Policy for Trade terms — tariffs, trade agreements, export restrictions.

TPU

Trade Policy Uncertainty

Trade × Policy × Uncertainty — regulatory risk specifically around trade.

EgPU

Energy Policy Uncertainty

The same method, pointed at energy markets and regulation.
